4 OBJECTIVES OF THE THESIS
The primary objective of this thesis is to examine the structural determinants of Risk
Management Effectiveness in Hybrid Project Environments, with particular emphasis on the
role of AI-Driven Strategic Risk Management. Hybrid project environments, characterized by
the coexistence of Agile and Waterfall methodologies, introduce structural complexity,
governance fragmentation, and dynamic risk interactions that challenge traditional risk
management approaches. This research seeks to empirically investigate how strategic
governance mechanisms and AI-driven analytical capabilities jointly influence effective risk
outcomes.
The specific objectives of the thesis are as follows:
1. To examine the impact of Risk Process Alignment between Agile and Waterfall
methodologies on Risk Management Effectiveness in hybrid project environments.
2. To analyse the influence of Risk Visibility Integration on Risk Management
Effectiveness in hybrid projects.
3. To assess the effect of Timely Risk Escalation on Risk Management Effectiveness in
hybrid project environments.
4. To evaluate the role of Governance Consistency in enhancing Risk Management
Effectiveness in hybrid projects.
5. To examine the impact of AI-Driven Risk Analytics Capability on overall Risk
Management Effectiveness in hybrid project environments using Structural Equation
Modelling (SEM).

Abstract
The increasing adoption of hybrid project methodologies integrating Agile adaptability with
Waterfall structure has introduced significant complexity into organizational risk governance.
Traditional risk management approaches, primarily reliant on manual assessment and static
control frameworks, often struggle to address the dynamic and fragmented nature of hybrid
environments. Concurrently, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as a strategic enabler of
predictive analytics, real-time monitoring, and data-driven decision support. However, limited
empirical research has examined how AI capability interacts with structural governance
mechanisms to influence overall risk management effectiveness in hybrid project settings.
This study investigates the structural determinants of Risk Management Effectiveness in
Hybrid Project Environments, with particular emphasis on AI-Driven Risk Analytics
Capability. A quantitative research design was employed using primary data collected from 384
project professionals across diverse industries. Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) was
utilized to examine the direct effects of five key dimensions: Risk Process Alignment, Risk
Visibility Integration, Timely Risk Escalation, Governance Consistency, and AI-Driven Risk
Analytics Capability. The findings reveal that Risk Process Alignment, Governance
Consistency, and AI-Driven Risk Analytics Capability have statistically significant and positive
effects on Risk Management Effectiveness. Among these, AI-Driven Risk Analytics Capability
emerged as one of the strongest predictors, indicating that advanced analytical intelligence
substantially enhances predictive foresight and decision quality in hybrid settings. In contrast,
Risk Visibility Integration demonstrated only a marginal direct effect, while Timely Risk
Escalation did not exhibit a statistically significant influence. These results suggest that
structural coherence and technological capability exert greater impact on risk outcomes than
operational transparency or escalation responsiveness alone. The study contributes to project
management and risk governance literature by positioning AI capability as a strategic structural
determinant rather than a peripheral technological tool. It advances hybrid project theory by
demonstrating that effectiveness depends on deliberate integration of governance consistency
and process alignment. Practically, the findings provide evidence-based guidance for managers
seeking to strengthen risk governance in complex project ecosystems. The research concludes
that sustainable risk performance in hybrid environments requires integrated structural
alignment, governance stability, and AI-driven analytical intelligence.

10 Introduction
The contemporary project landscape is undergoing a profound transformation driven by
technological advancement, organizational globalization, and increasing environmental
volatility. Projects today are no longer isolated, linear undertakings; instead, they are complex
socio-technical systems embedded within dynamic organizational and external environments.
This complexity has significantly increased project exposure to uncertainty, making risk an
inherent and continuous feature across the project lifecycle rather than a discrete event
addressed at predefined stages (Flyvbjerg, 2017; Williams et al., 2010).
Despite the centrality of risk in modern projects, traditional project risk management practices
remain largely rooted in deterministic planning paradigms. These practices typically rely on
static risk registers, periodic assessments, and subjective expert judgment, which often fail to
capture evolving risk interactions, emergent threats, and non-linear causal relationships (Ward
& Chapman, 2003; Aven, 2016). As project environments become more data-intensive and
time-sensitive, such approaches increasingly struggle to provide timely, accurate, and
actionable insights for decision-makers.
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as a critical enabler capable of addressing these
limitations by fundamentally redefining how project risks are identified, assessed, and
managed. Rather than serving as a standalone automation tool, AI represents a paradigm shift
toward adaptive, learning-based, and evidence-driven risk governance. By leveraging machine
learning, predictive analytics, and intelligent monitoring systems, AI enables continuous
sensing of project conditions, early detection of weak risk signals, and dynamic updating of
risk profiles in real time (Jordan & Mitchell, 2015; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).
AI-driven analytical models are particularly effective in environments characterized by large
volumes of heterogeneous data. Project data—ranging from schedules, budgets, and
10

performance metrics to communication logs and stakeholder feedback—can be integrated and
analyzed to uncover latent patterns and interdependencies that are beyond human cognitive
capacity (Provost & Fawcett, 2013; Biesenthal et al., 2022). Predictive models built on such
data can forecast schedule slippages, cost escalations, and performance degradation with
greater accuracy than traditional rule-based methods, enabling earlier and more targeted risk
mitigation interventions (Zhang et al., 2021; Shao et al., 2025).
From a decision-theoretic perspective, AI-based risk management systems address inherent
limitations of human judgment, including bounded rationality, cognitive bias, and information
overload (Simon, 1997; Kahneman, 2011). While human expertise remains essential for
contextual interpretation and ethical judgment, AI enhances decision quality by providing
consistent, scalable, and data-driven insights. This synergy between human and artificial
intelligence has given rise to hybrid decision-making models that improve both the speed and
robustness of risk-related decisions in complex projects (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021; Hanaysha
et al., 2021).
The growing maturity of AI technologies has accelerated their adoption in large-scale and
mission-critical projects. Organizations operating in sectors such as manufacturing, finance,
aerospace, and public infrastructure increasingly embed AI-based systems within their project
governance frameworks to enhance visibility, automate risk monitoring, and optimize response
strategies (Bughin et al., 2018; Treacy et al., 2025). These developments indicate a shift from
experimental adoption toward institutionalized use of AI in project risk management, signaling
its strategic importance for organizational resilience and competitiveness.
At the same time, the integration of AI into project management raises important questions
regarding governance, transparency, and managerial control. Effective use of AI requires
alignment between technological capabilities, organizational processes, and decision authority
structures. Without such alignment, AI systems risk becoming opaque tools that generate
outputs without meaningful integration into managerial workflows (Marnewick et al., 2020;
Dwivedi et al., 2023). Consequently, understanding how AI-enabled risk management operates
within real project contexts has become a critical area of scholarly inquiry.

10.1 Background and Significance of Artificial Intelligence in IT Project Management
The relevance of AI is particularly pronounced in the context of IT project management, where
uncertainty, complexity, and rapid change are defining characteristics. IT projects often involve
evolving requirements, fast-paced technological innovation, and interdependent system
architectures, making them highly susceptible to risks related to scope creep, integration
failure, and resource misalignment (Sauer et al., 2007; Standish Group, 2020). Historical
evidence consistently shows that a significant proportion of IT projects fail to meet their
original objectives, underscoring the need for more advanced and adaptive risk management
approaches.
Traditional IT project management frameworks emphasize upfront planning and periodic
control mechanisms, which may be insufficient in environments where requirements and
technologies evolve continuously. Artificial Intelligence introduces a fundamentally different
approach by enabling continuous, real-time decision support across the entire project lifecycle.
Through the application of machine learning, natural language processing (NLP), and advanced
analytics, AI systems assist project managers in interpreting complex information streams and
anticipating future project states with greater accuracy (Wamba et al., 2021; Siau & Wang,
2018).
One of the most significant contributions of AI to IT project management lies in its ability to
integrate structured and unstructured data sources. Structured data, such as schedules, budgets,
and defect metrics, can be combined with unstructured data, including emails, meeting
transcripts, and issue reports, to generate a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of
project risk exposure (Provost & Fawcett, 2013; Biesenthal et al., 2022). This holistic data
integration enables earlier detection of emerging risks and supports proactive rather than
reactive management strategies.
AI systems further enhance IT project risk management through adaptive learning capabilities.
As projects progress and new data become available, AI models can update their predictions
and recommendations, maintaining relevance in dynamic environments. This continuous
learning feature is particularly valuable in IT projects, where changing stakeholder expectations
and technological dependencies can rapidly alter risk profiles (March, 1991; Zhang et al.,
2021).
In addition to analytical improvements, AI has reshaped coordination and communication
mechanisms within IT project teams. AI-enabled dashboards, intelligent assistants, and

automated reporting tools facilitate real-time information sharing and improve transparency
across geographically distributed teams (Fountaine et al., 2019). Enhanced visibility and
communication are critical for managing interdependencies and ensuring timely escalation of
risks in complex IT projects.
From a strategic and sustainability perspective, AI-enabled risk management contributes to
long-term organizational resilience. Sustainable project outcomes depend not only on meeting
immediate performance targets but also on managing risks that threaten long-term value
creation, social responsibility, and resource efficiency (Elkington, 1997; United Nations, 2015).
By improving risk anticipation, decision quality, and adaptive capacity, AI strengthens
organizational ability to navigate uncertainty and supports more sustainable and responsible IT
project execution (Aven & Renn, 2010)
10.2 Theoretical Background
10.2.1 Definition of Project Risk Management
Project risk management is a systematic and structured process that aims to identify, assess,
and manage risks that may affect project results. It is a process that enables the measurement
of risks and the development of strategies to mitigate risks and avoid undesirable outcomes
(Faruk, 2025). (Kaplan & Garrick, 1981) describe risk as a product of the probability of
occurrence of an event and its potential consequences. This is a quantitative method that
enables organizations to manage risks and develop strategies to avoid disruptions.
An effective risk management process includes a number of key elements, such as risk
identification, risk analysis, risk response planning, and risk monitoring. Risk identification is
the process of identifying potential risks that may impede the success of a project, while risk
analysis is the process of determining the risk potential of each risk situation. After the risks
have been analysed, risk response plans can be developed, which may include risk avoidance,
risk reduction, risk transfer, or risk acceptance.
10.2.2 AI technologies Applicable to Risk Management
The incorporation of artificial intelligence (AI) in risk management has revolutionized the way
risks are identified, analysed and mitigated. AI-based risk management tools are more efficient,
accurate, and scalable than their conventional counterparts, allowing organizations to
effectively mitigate risks in real-time. AI-based risk assessment employs a range of advanced
13

=== PAGE 14 ===
technologies to enhance predictive accuracy and automate risk mitigation plans (Dutta, D., &
Bose, 2015). The primary AI technologies that can be leveraged for risk management are:
Machine Learning (ML) – Machine learning algorithms are used to analyse large datasets to
detect patterns and predict risks, and then suggest ways to mitigate them. Machine learning
models improve their accuracy levels by learning from new data, making them very effective
in detecting anomalies, predicting system failures, and identifying vulnerabilities in critical
infrastructure systems.
Natural Language Processing (NLP) – NLP technologies help AI systems to derive meaningful
insights from unstructured text sources like security updates, intelligence meetings, social
media updates, and live communications. By analysing language patterns, NLP-based AI
solutions can detect potential threats, point out misinformation campaigns, and offer warning
signs for potential risks.
Deep Learning (DL) – Deep learning algorithms, especially neural networks, improve risk
detection abilities by processing unstructured data, like images, videos, and sensor inputs. Deep
learning models are commonly employed in domains such as biometric security, surveillance
analytics, and automated anomaly detection in cybersecurity solutions (Wenzel, M., Krause,
B., & Wagner, 2022).
Through the integration of these AI technologies, risk management frameworks can be made
more adaptable, precise, and responsive. AI-based risk assessment tools are capable of handling
large amounts of diverse data, thus minimizing the possibility of human error and allowing
organizations to shift from a reactive to a proactive risk management strategy. The ability of
AI to quickly analyse risk scenarios, simulate risks, and provide recommendations on the best
risk mitigation strategies makes it a crucial component of risk governance frameworks.
Theoretical background of risk management offers the required context to appreciate the role
of AI in improving risk identification, mitigation, and response strategies. Conventional risk
management strategies, although helpful, have become less effective in dealing with the
dynamic risks that critical infrastructure and national security projects are exposed to. The
adoption of AI technologies such as ML, NLP, and DL has brought about a paradigm shift in
risk management strategies, enabling real-time analysis of threats and automated decision
support. As AI technology evolves, its application in risk management will become more
prominent, leading to improvements in resilience, efficiency, and overall security in high-risk
sectors.

10.3 Problem Statement
The rapid evolution of project environments particularly the rise of hybrid methodologies
combining Agile flexibility with Waterfall structure has significantly altered the nature of risk
management. While traditional project risk management literature has extensively examined
structured risk identification, qualitative and quantitative assessment methods, and governance
frameworks, most studies are grounded in conventional predictive environments. These studies
emphasize documentation, control mechanisms, and hierarchical escalation structures but do
not adequately address the procedural complexity introduced when adaptive and predictive
methodologies coexist within the same project ecosystem.
In parallel, a growing body of research has explored the application of Artificial Intelligence
(AI) in project management and risk governance. These studies primarily focus on machine
learning for cost prediction, predictive analytics for schedule overruns, anomaly detection in
IT systems, and automation of risk monitoring dashboards. Although this body of literature
demonstrates the technological potential of AI in improving forecasting accuracy and
operational efficiency, it largely treats AI as a standalone technological enhancement rather
than as an integrated strategic capability embedded within broader governance and procedural
systems.
A critical limitation in existing scholarship is the lack of empirical integration between
structural governance mechanisms and AI-driven analytical capabilities within hybrid project
contexts. Most prior studies examine either (1) traditional risk management practices without
AI integration, or (2) AI-based analytics without embedding them within formal governance
structures and aligned risk processes. There remains a significant absence of comprehensive
structural models that simultaneously test how procedural alignment, governance consistency,
operational visibility, escalation mechanisms, and AI-driven analytics interact to influence
overall risk management effectiveness.
Moreover, the majority of empirical investigations into AI-enabled risk management rely on
descriptive case studies, conceptual frameworks, or technology adoption surveys. Very few
studies employ rigorous multivariate statistical approaches particularly Structural Equation
Modelling (SEM) to examine causal relationships among governance structures, process
alignment, AI capability, and performance outcomes. The absence of SEM-based validation
limits the ability to understand not merely whether AI contributes to risk management, but how
15

=== PAGE 16 ===
it structurally interacts with governance and process mechanisms to produce measurable
effectiveness.
Hybrid project environments further intensify this gap. By design, hybrid methodologies
integrate Agile adaptability with Waterfall predictability. While this integration offers
flexibility and control, it also introduces structural fragmentation. Agile teams often operate
with decentralized decision-making, iterative planning cycles, and lightweight documentation,
whereas Waterfall components rely on formal stage-gates, structured risk registers, and
hierarchical reporting channels. This methodological duality can create inconsistencies in risk
documentation, ambiguity in escalation authority, duplication of monitoring mechanisms, and
misalignment in governance oversight. Without deliberate structural harmonization, risk
management processes may operate in parallel silos rather than as a coherent system.
Existing research has not sufficiently examined how AI capability functions within such
fragmented environments. It remains unclear whether AI-driven analytics independently
improves risk outcomes, or whether its effectiveness depends on aligned processes and
consistent governance frameworks. Similarly, the relative contribution of structural factors
(such as governance consistency and process alignment) versus operational mechanisms (such
as visibility and escalation) has not been empirically disentangled in hybrid contexts.
Therefore, a significant research gap exists at the intersection of AI capability, strategic risk
governance, and hybrid project environments. There is a need for an integrated empirical model
that simultaneously examines multiple structural dimensions of risk governance and evaluates
their direct influence on risk management effectiveness using robust statistical techniques.
Specifically, the literature lacks:
1. Empirical SEM-based validation of AI-driven strategic risk management models in
hybrid project settings.
2. Integrated structural testing of AI capability alongside governance consistency and risk
process alignment.
3. Comparative assessment of structural versus operational risk mechanisms within hybrid
environments.
4. Evidence clarifying how fragmentation inherent in hybrid methodologies influences
risk governance effectiveness.

10.6 Significance of the Study
The significance of this study lies in its contribution to advancing theoretical understanding,
improving managerial practice, informing policy considerations, and clarifying the governance
implications of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in hybrid project environments. As organizations
increasingly adopt hybrid methodologies and integrate AI-driven tools into risk governance
systems, there is a growing need for empirically grounded insights into how structural and
technological dimensions collectively influence risk management effectiveness.
10.6.1 Academic Significance
From an academic perspective, this study contributes to the project management and risk
governance literature in several important ways. First, it extends traditional project risk
management theory by integrating AI-Driven Risk Analytics Capability as a structural
determinant of risk management effectiveness rather than treating AI as merely a technological
tool. Second, it advances hybrid project management research by empirically examining how
procedural alignment and governance consistency operate within environments that combine
Agile adaptability and Waterfall structure.
Furthermore, the study addresses a methodological gap by employing Structural Equation
Modelling (SEM) to test an integrated framework that simultaneously evaluates multiple
dimensions of strategic risk management. Previous research has largely examined these factors
in isolation or through descriptive approaches. By validating a comprehensive structural model,
the study strengthens theoretical clarity regarding the relative influence of process alignment,
governance mechanisms, visibility integration, escalation responsiveness, and AI capability on
risk performance outcomes.
10.6.2 Practical Significance
Practically, the findings provide evidence-based guidance for project managers, PMO leaders,
risk officers, and organizational executives operating in hybrid project environments. The study
identifies which structural dimensions most strongly influence risk management effectiveness,
enabling decision-makers to prioritize investments and interventions accordingly.

12 2. Literature Review
The effective management of risks is widely recognized as a critical determinant of project
success, particularly in complex and dynamic environments where uncertainty, stakeholder
demands, and regulatory requirements converge. Traditional project management approaches,
such as the Waterfall methodology, emphasize structured planning and linear execution,
providing predictability but often lacking flexibility to respond to emergent risks, while agile
methodologies offer adaptability and iterative processes yet face limitations in large-scale
infrastructure projects. Hybrid project management, which integrates the predictability of
traditional approaches with the responsiveness of Agile, has emerged as a practical solution to
address these challenges, particularly in infrastructure and large-scale projects. Concurrently,
the advent of artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed risk management by enabling
predictive analytics, real-time monitoring, and data-driven decision-making, enhancing the
accuracy and effectiveness of risk mitigation strategies.
This literature review critically examines the evolution of project risk management, the
theoretical foundations of strategic risk governance, and the role of AI-enabled tools and
methodologies in enhancing risk management effectiveness within hybrid project
environments, highlighting key enablers, gaps, and opportunities for research-driven
improvements.
12.1 Overview of Risk Management in Project Management
Project risk management (PRM) is essential for project success, focusing on identifying,
analyzing, and mitigating risks throughout a project's lifecycle. It employs systematic
approaches, integrating risk assessment and mitigation strategies, alongside ongoing
monitoring to reduce uncertainties. According to (Okafor, 2021) effective PRM involves core
principles, methodologies, and best practices, emphasizing the role of risk management tools,
stakeholder engagement, and structured decision-making frameworks. Case studies highlighted
in the research demonstrate that proactive risk management prevents cost overruns, schedule
delays, and quality issues, stressing the importance of integrating risk management into project
planning and execution to enhance resilience and sustainability.

12.2 Strategic Risk Management: Theoretical Foundations
Strategic risk management (SRM) extends conventional risk management by integrating risk
considerations into organizational strategy and governance frameworks. SRM emphasizes
aligning risk policies with strategic objectives, promoting executive oversight, performance
monitoring, and risk-informed decision-making. The theoretical foundations of SRM are
derived from Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) theory, governance theory, dynamic
capability theory, and the resource-based view (RBV). These frameworks collectively
highlight the importance of proactive risk governance in sustaining organizational resilience
and competitive advantage.

12.2.1 Hybrid Project Management in Infrastructure Projects
Project management has historically relied on structured, sequential models, particularly the
Waterfall approach, to manage large-scale infrastructure developments. These methods
emphasize upfront planning, scope definition, detailed scheduling, and linear execution,
offering predictability and control (Kerzner, 2023). In infrastructure projects where physical
deliverables, fixed budgets, and regulatory scrutiny dominate such predictability is crucial.
However, rigid models often fail to accommodate mid-project changes, emerging risks, or
stakeholder demands, particularly in volatile economic or environmental conditions (Hosseini
et al., 2023).
The limitations of traditional methods in dynamic environments have fueled interest in more
adaptive approaches. Agile project management, developed for fast paced software
development environments, is characterized by short iteration cycles, frequent customer
feedback, minimal upfront planning, and collaborative team structures (Hoda & Murugesan,
2023). Despite these advantages, the direct application of Agile in infrastructure is constrained
by dependencies on physical assets, strict compliance requirements, and longer project
durations (Khan & Al-Shammari, 2023).
Hybrid project management methodologies emerged as a practical response to the
incompatibility of pure Agile in non-software environments. These approaches aim to combine
the adaptability of Agile with the structure and control of traditional models (Praveen, 2025).
In large-scale infrastructure settings, hybrid methodologies typically apply Agile principles in
design, planning, or stakeholder communication phases, while maintaining traditional controls
in procurement, construction, and regulatory compliance. Research by (Bjorvatn & Wald,
2023) emphasizes that hybrid approaches are particularly suited for projects operating in
turbulent or uncertain conditions, where flexibility is needed but must be balanced with
governance. Similarly, (Denicol et al., 2022) assert that the organizational architecture of

megaprojects supports hybrid configurations, with core teams functioning iteratively while
broader functions follow established protocols.
12.3 Traditional vs. AI-Driven Risk Management
Traditional risk management techniques and AI-driven risk management approaches
significantly differ in their methods of data handling processes, analysis, and decision-making.
In the traditional approach, risk management heavily depends on manual efforts to gather and
scrutinize data from records and rely on insights from experts (Lundqvist, 2015). In contrast,
AI-driven risk management exploits machine learning and sophisticated algorithm methods to
effectively examine and evaluate immense volumes of data (Zigiene et al., 2019). AI systems
can decipher complex data patterns, assess correlations, and forecast real-time potential hazards
empowering organizations to react promptly and efficiently. Traditional approaches may
struggle to scale and adapt to changing risk landscapes (Hopkin, 2018). Whereas AI-driven
systems are highly scalable and adaptable. Traditional risk management relies on human
judgments and familiarities, low initial investment, adaptability, and subjectivity; however, it
may have limitations in scalability, response time, and predictive analysis. On the other hand,
AI-driven risk management offers advanced analytics, predictive capabilities, automation, real-
time insights, and a reduced potential for bias, but it may require greater technical expertise
and raise concerns about data privacy and overreliance on technology (Schuett, 2023). Overall,
AI-driven methods prioritize advanced analytics and automation, while traditional approaches
are based on human judgment and familiarity. However, each of these approaches offers
distinct advantages and complications in managing risks.
12.4 AI Techniques for Risk Management Practices
AI technologies have enhanced risk management practices by improving accuracy and
efficiency in cost modelling, time forecasting, and risk modelling. These approaches allow
organizations to leverage historical and real-time data to inform decisions, optimize resource
allocation, and proactively mitigate risks.
12.4.1 Cost Modelling Methods
Cost modelling methods are compassed through a three-stage model comprising input, process,

12.4.2 Time Modelling Methods
AI approaches for risk management processes contain a range of time modelling methods that
attempt to predict the timing and duration of prospective risks accurately. These techniques
deploy AI algorithms to evaluate past data, determine trends, and predict the timing of
upcoming occurrences (Salehi & Burgueño, 2018). A time series analysis is a commonly
employed methodology that entails the examination of sequential datasets in order to detect
patterns, seasonal variations, and anomalies. Time modelling in risk management often
involves the utilization of techniques such as autoregressive integrated moving averages
(ARIMA) and seasonal decomposition of time series (STL) (He et al., 2022) (Schaffer et al.,
2021). Furthermore, machine learning methods, such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and
long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, possess the capability to effectively capture
temporal relationships within datasets, enabling them to generate predictions pertaining to
forthcoming time points (Sherstinsky, 2020). Top management can improve their capacities to
anticipate and mitigate risks that occur over time by integrating time modelling methodologies
into their risk management procedures, resulting in improved strategic planning and decision-
making.

management practices, namely in the context of risk modelling procedures. These techniques
utilize sophisticated algorithms and machine learning methods to analyse massive amounts of

12.6 Hypothesis Development
12.6.1 Impact of Risk Process Alignment on Risk Management Effectiveness in Hybrid
Project Environments
Risk process alignment refers to the consistent integration and synchronization of risk
management activities such as risk identification, analysis, response planning, and monitoring
across both Agile and Waterfall methodologies within hybrid project environments. Hybrid
methodologies combine the structured planning of Waterfall with the iterative flexibility of
Agile to address complex project requirements. Research shows that hybrid risk management
enhances resilience and delivers superior project outcomes by blending proactive risk
identification with adaptive responses, provided that risk processes are aligned across
methodologies (Leech, 2025).
Moreover, evidence suggests that misalignment between sequential planning and iterative
delivery often leads to inconsistent risk practices, resulting in duplication, oversight gaps, and
reactive risk responses that undermine project objectives (Ibrahim & Bezverkhyi, 2024).
Empirical studies also point out that hybrid frameworks achieve higher risk responsiveness and
stakeholder satisfaction when methodological boundaries are bridged through aligned risk
processes (Praveen, 2025).

=== PAGE 29 ===
Additionally, the literature on hybrid risk management emphasizes that alignment of
governance mechanisms and risk workflows across project phases improves visibility, reduces
escalation delays, and enhances decision quality (Székely et al., 2025). Collectively, these
studies indicate that when risk process alignment between Agile and Waterfall is achieved,
hybrid projects are better positioned to handle uncertainty and improve risk mitigation
effectiveness. Based on the reviewed literature, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H1: Risk process alignment has a significant positive impact on risk management effectiveness
in hybrid project environments.
12.6.2 Influence of Risk Visibility Integration on Risk Management Effectiveness
Risk visibility integration denotes the practice of consolidating risk information from both
Agile and Waterfall components into unified dashboards, reports, and communication channels
so that all stakeholders have access to up-to-date risk status and analytics. Research
increasingly highlights that higher risk visibility enhances decision-making efficiency in
complex projects by enabling early identification of threats and facilitating coordinated
mitigation across teams. For instance, risk visibility allows cross-functional teams to detect
emerging risks before they escalate, creating opportunities for rapid alignment of mitigation
actions (Jacob, 2025).
Literature on hybrid project governance emphasizes that real-time risk tracking and integrated
reporting systems improve collaboration between agile teams and formal planners, thus
reducing ambiguity and information silos that typically impair hybrid risk management
effectiveness (Mirzaei et al., 2025). Furthermore, studies show that enhanced visibility of risk
metrics across project stages increases transparency, fosters accountability, and supports
informed strategic interventions.
Finally, research on automated risk analytics and dashboards argues that tools which integrate
risk signals from different methodologies are associated with improved responsiveness and
reduced negative project outcomes, particularly in hybrid contexts where disparate teams
coexist (Nwosu & Peter, 2025). Therefore, the following hypothesis has been developed:
H2: Risk visibility integration has a significant positive impact on risk management
effectiveness in hybrid project environments.

12.6.3 Effect of Timely Risk Escalation on Risk Management Effectiveness
Timely risk escalation refers to the rapid elevation of critical risks to decision-making
authorities or governance bodies once certain thresholds (e.g., cost impact, schedule variance,
regulatory exposure) are breached. In project environments where Agile iterations and
Waterfall milestones coexist, the delay in escalation often results in response lag, reduced
mitigation window, and weakened control over strategic risks. Recent studies show that
structured escalation protocols tied to predefined risk triggers significantly improve oversight
and proactive governance, enabling leadership to intervene before risks cause cascading
disruptions (Jacob, 2025).
Research on risk governance in hybrid settings further supports that escalation thresholds and
formal notification mechanisms improve alignment between cross-team processes, ensuring
that high-impact risks receive priority intervention (Székely et al., 2025). Comparative analyses
demonstrate that projects with timely escalation systems outperform those with ad-hoc
escalation practices across metrics such as schedule adherence and stakeholder satisfaction.
Moreover, real-time risk management literature argues that escalation mechanisms embedded
within automated analytics alert systems enable more rapid responses to risk deviations,
resulting in improved resilience and reduced negative outcomes. Based on the reviewed
literature, the following hypothesis is proposed:
H3: Timely risk escalation has a significant positive impact on risk management effectiveness
in hybrid project environments.
12.6.4 Role of Governance Consistency in Enhancing Risk Management Effectiveness
Governance consistency refers to the uniform application of policies, standards, roles, and
decision rights across the hybrid project lifecycle. Consistent governance reduces ambiguity,
ensures adherence to risk thresholds, and aligns risk oversight across methodological
boundaries. Recent systematic research on hybrid risk governance emphasizes that
standardized governance frameworks improve accountability, risk ownership, and integration
of cross-team processes (Ibrahim & Bezverkhyi, 2024).
Additionally, literature on project risk and governance highlights that when governance
structures incorporate both predictive and adaptive risk controls, risk responses are

Finally, research on digital risk management frameworks shows that governance consistency
ensures that automated systems and manual processes adhere to shared risk policies, leading to
fewer gaps and more cohesive mitigation strategies (Kapri, 2023). Based on the reviewed

H4: Governance consistency has a significant positive impact on risk management

12.7 Research Gap
Despite the extensive literature on project risk management (PRM) and strategic risk
management (SRM), several gaps remain in understanding how these practices function within
hybrid project environments that combine Agile and Waterfall methodologies. Existing studies
predominantly focus on traditional risk management approaches or general AI applications in
project risk contexts, with limited empirical evidence on how AI-driven risk analytics
integrates with hybrid risk governance to enhance effectiveness. While prior research has
highlighted the benefits of risk process alignment, visibility integration, timely escalation, and
governance consistency, most studies have examined these factors in isolation, often within
either fully Agile or fully Waterfall projects. Furthermore, although AI techniques such as
neural networks, machine learning algorithms, and predictive analytics have been explored for
cost, time, and risk modelling, there is a paucity of research investigating their practical
implementation and impact on decision-making across hybrid project phases. The literature
also indicates limited attention to how AI-enabled risk management supports sustainable
decision-making and organizational resilience in dynamic and complex project environments.
Therefore, a comprehensive empirical investigation that integrates AI-driven capabilities with
hybrid risk management practices addressing alignment, visibility, escalation, and governance
is needed to fill these gaps and provide actionable insights for enhancing risk management
effectiveness in hybrid projects.

Variables
Risk Process Alignment
Risk process alignment refers to the degree to which risk management processes are
consistently structured and integrated across different project methodologies (such as Agile
and Waterfall). It involves aligning risk identification, assessment, response planning,
implementation, and monitoring processes so they work coherently across hybrid project
environments, enabling smoother coordination and decision-making.
Risk Visibility Integration
Risk visibility integration is the extent to which risk information is consolidated, shared, and
accessible across stakeholders and project layers, enabling continuous awareness of risk

exposures and facilitating timely decision-making. It includes transparent documentation,
dashboards, and communication channels that integrate risk data between teams, tools, and
governance structures (Schön et al., 2020).
Timely Risk Escalation
Timely risk escalation refers to the prompt reporting and escalation of risk events or triggers to

strategic intervention. This ensures that critical risks are addressed before they evolve into
significant issues impacting objectives.
Governance Consistency
Governance consistency denotes the uniform application of risk governance structures,

consistent oversight mechanisms, reporting lines, and accountability for risk activities to ensure
strategic alignment and compliance with organizational risk frameworks.
AI-Driven Risk Analytics Capability
AI-driven risk analytics capability refers to the organizational ability to apply artificial
intelligence, machine learning, and advanced analytics methods to detect, analyse, predict, and
visualize risk patterns from structured and unstructured data. This capability enhances
predictive insight into future risks, automates risk detection, supports prioritization, and
provides actionable recommendations.
Risk Management Effectiveness in Hybrid Projects
Risk management effectiveness in hybrid projects refers to the degree to which risk
management practices successfully achieve desired outcomes minimizing negative impacts on
project objectives (scope, cost, time, quality) while enabling opportunities through systematic
identification, evaluation, response, monitoring, and control of risks within hybrid project
environments (combined Agile and Waterfall).

KMO and Bartlett's Test
The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) Test gauges how well your data fits into the Factor Analysis
framework. The test evaluates sampling adequacy for the whole model as well as for each of
the variables. The percentage of variation among variables that may represent common
variance is measured by the statistic. Your data is better suited for factor analysis if the
percentage is smaller.
KMO gives values in the range of 0 and 1. A general guideline for deciphering the statistic:
• KMO values in the range of 0.8 and 1 show that the sampling is sufficient.
• KMO values below 0.6 suggest that corrective action is necessary since the sample is
insufficient. Use your own discretion for values between 0.5 and 0.6, since some writers
have set this value at 0.5.
• Large partial correlations relative to the total of correlations are indicated by KMO
values around zero. Stated differently, the prevalence of correlations poses a significant
challenge to component analysis.

The Reliability and Validity of the scale:
Ensuring the reliability and validity of the scale is crucial in research. To guarantee that the

Reliability
Reliability refers to the consistency of a measurement. A reliable instrument consistently
produces the same results when research is repeated under identical conditions. For example,
if a thermometer consistently gives the same temperature reading for a liquid under identical
conditions across multiple tests, the results are considered reliable.
Factor loading
The degree of correlation between an observable variable and a latent variable is indicated by
a number called the factor loading. It usually ranges from 0 to 1, however when computing
many variables, certain datasets may have factor loadings larger than 1. Greater relationship
with the latent variable is indicated by higher factor loadings. For instance, the latent variable
of anxiety has a factor loading of 0.85 for question one and 0.33 for question two, according to
a study of survey data. Question one is likely more successful than Question 2 in identifying
respondents who suffer from anxiety since it has a higher factor loading. This is because
question one has a larger correlation with the latent variable than question two, according to
the factor loading (Team, 2024).
Cronbach’s alpha:
The Cronbach alpha coefficient method is the most commonly used to measure the reliability
of a study. This coefficient ranges from 0 to 1. Many studies suggest that a minimum score of
0.6 indicates reliability, while a score above 0.7 is often regarded as high reliability. However,
a reliability score in the range of 0.5 to 0.6 is acceptable in the early stages of basic research
specifically at pilot study stage.
In this research, Cronbach's alpha has been used to investigate for data reliability. It is a widely
used tool in marketing research. A low alpha value indicates that the items do not adequately

Discriminant Validity test
This validity shows that there are no superfluous items in the measurement model of a
construct. By using a discrepancy measure known as Modification Indices (MI), AMOS was
able to determine which items in the model were redundant. A high MI value suggests that the
corresponding items are unnecessary. The measurement model could be run after the researcher
removes one of the identified items. The redundant pair could also be constrained by the
researcher using a "free parameter estimate." A further prerequisite for discriminant validity is
that there should be no more than 0.85 correlation between exogenous constructs. When the
correlation value is greater than 0.85, it means that there is a significant multicollinearity issue
or that the two exogenous constructs are redundant (Awang, 2014).

Confirmatory component Analysis (CFA) is an advanced statistical method used to validate the
component structure of a collection of observed data. It enables researchers to evaluate the

hypothesis that a correlation between observable variables and their underlying latent
constructs is present. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) contrasts with Exploratory Factor
Analysis (EFA), since the former tests a predetermined structure of the data rather than
discovering it.
The main objective of CFA is to ascertain the extent to which the data aligns with a proposed
measuring model derived from theoretical frameworks or previous studies. This entails many
essential steps:
• Defining constructions: The method starts with the precise delineation of the theoretical
constructions. At this point, researchers often pretest the elements of the construct to
assess their clarity and confirm their appropriate representation of the notion.
• Formulating the Measurement Model: In Confirmatory component Analysis (CFA),
researchers define the principle of one-dimensionality, whereby many observed
variables exclusively assess a singular component or construct. Generally, an effective
approach entails possessing a minimum of three things for each build.
• Defining the Model: Researchers must delineate the number of factors and the
configuration of loadings (which variables correspond to which components). This
specification is based on theoretical anticipations or outcomes from prior research.
• Evaluating Model Fit: The validity of the measurement model is determined by
juxtaposing the theoretical model with the empirical data. This entails analyzing factor
loadings (with a typical threshold of 0.7 or above for sufficient loadings) and fit indices
like Chi-square, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Goodness of
Fit Index (GFI), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI).

16.2 Discriminant Validity
Discriminant validity is not a specific test performed in SPSS or any other statistical software
but a concept within the context of validating measurement instruments and assessing the
relationships between variables. Discriminant validity is crucial to ensure that different
constructs or variables in a study are truly distinct and not measuring the same underlying
concept. Researchers use various techniques such as confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) or
correlation analysis to demonstrate that the measures intended to assess different constructs
are, indeed, different and not highly correlated. Discriminant validity helps ensure that the
measurement instruments accurately represent the unique concepts they are meant to measure,
preventing construct overlap or redundancy and allowing for more robust and accurate data
analysis and interpretation.

16.3 SEM (Structural Equational Modelling)
Structural Equation Modelling (SEM), a flexible statistical approach, to describe complex
interactions between variables, whether latent or observable. Its ability to analyse intricate
causal pathways, integrate latent components, test several hypotheses at once, account for
measurement error, evaluate model fit, and combine aspects of factor analysis and regression
are just a few of its special features. SEM is an essential tool for research in disciplines like
psychology, sociology, economics, and beyond because it can be used to validate theoretical
models, examine the effects of interventions or policies, and simplify complex datasets. This
allows for more thorough and accurate data analysis and hypothesis testing.

18 DISCUSSION
The purpose of this study was to examine how governance mechanisms and artificial
intelligence–enabled analytics influence project risk management effectiveness in hybrid
project environments. Using structural equation modeling, the research evaluated the relative
contributions of governance consistency, risk process alignment, risk visibility, timely risk
escalation, and AI analytics capability. The findings reveal that risk management effectiveness
in hybrid projects is driven primarily by structural coherence and organizational capability
rather than by information availability or escalation speed alone. This highlights an important
shift in how risk is governed in complex project settings that combine Agile and traditional
methodologies.
One of the most significant findings is the strong positive effect of governance consistency on
risk management effectiveness. Hybrid projects frequently operate under dual governance
logics, where Agile teams emphasize autonomy and flexibility while traditional structures rely
on formal controls and hierarchical oversight. When these governance systems are
inconsistently applied, ambiguity emerges around accountability, decision rights, and
escalation pathways, increasing the likelihood of unmanaged risks. The results of this study
indicate that consistent governance across hybrid structures reduces such ambiguity and
enhances coordination, supporting existing governance and agency theory perspectives that
emphasize the importance of clarity in roles and authority. This suggests that effective risk
management in hybrid projects requires deliberate integration of governance mechanisms
rather than parallel or fragmented control systems.
Risk process alignment also demonstrated a significant positive relationship with risk
management effectiveness, reinforcing the importance of structural integration across the
project lifecycle. Hybrid environments often exhibit misaligned risk practices, with Agile
teams relying on informal, iterative approaches and traditional teams adhering to formalized
risk registers and stage-gate reviews. The findings suggest that aligning these processes into a
coherent risk management framework enables organizations to identify, assess, and respond to
risks more effectively. From a dynamic capabilities perspective, this alignment enhances an
organization’s ability to sense and adapt to uncertainty by ensuring that risk information flows
78

=== PAGE 79 ===
consistently across teams and decision levels. This reinforces prior research that identifies
process integration as a critical success factor in complex project environments.
In contrast, risk visibility did not demonstrate a statistically significant direct effect on risk
management effectiveness. While prior studies often emphasize transparency and information
sharing as key enablers of effective risk management, the results of this study suggest that
visibility alone is insufficient in hybrid project contexts. One possible explanation is that
increased visibility does not necessarily lead to improved decision-making if governance
structures and interpretive mechanisms are weak. In hybrid projects, risk information may be
widely available but not effectively prioritized or acted upon due to competing decision logics
or unclear accountability. Additionally, Agile environments generate high volumes of real-time
data, which can contribute to information overload rather than enhanced situational awareness.
These findings indicate that risk visibility functions more as an enabling condition that supports
other governance mechanisms, rather than as an independent driver of effectiveness.
Similarly, timely risk escalation was not found to have a significant direct impact on risk
management effectiveness. This challenges traditional assumptions that rapid escalation is
inherently beneficial in managing project risks. In hybrid project environments, Agile
principles encourage decentralized decision-making and local problem resolution, reducing the
need for frequent formal escalation. Rapid escalation of risks that can be managed at the team
level may create unnecessary disruption, increase administrative burden, and undermine team
autonomy. The results suggest that escalation effectiveness in hybrid projects depends more on
appropriateness and quality than on speed. This aligns with emerging literature that argues for
context-sensitive escalation policies, particularly in environments characterized by high
uncertainty and adaptive workflows.
AI analytics capability emerged as one of the strongest predictors of risk management
effectiveness, underscoring its growing strategic importance in contemporary project
management. Rather than functioning solely as a technical tool, AI analytics appears to operate
as an organizational capability that enhances predictive foresight and decision quality. By
continuously analyzing large volumes of project data, AI systems enable early detection of
emerging risks and support proactive decision-making. From a resource-based view, AI
analytics capability represents a valuable and difficult-to-imitate resource that can strengthen
an organization’s risk governance capacity. Importantly, the findings suggest that AI does not

replace traditional governance mechanisms but amplifies their effectiveness by reducing
cognitive limitations and improving situational awareness within structured risk processes.
Taken together, the findings indicate that effective risk management in hybrid projects is
shaped by the interaction between governance structures, aligned processes, and advanced
analytical capabilities. Structural coherence provides the foundation for effective risk
governance, while AI analytics enhances an organization’s ability to adapt to uncertainty. The
non-significant effects of risk visibility and escalation highlight that risk management
effectiveness is not determined by information availability or response speed alone, but by the
organization’s capacity to interpret information and make coordinated decisions. This
integrated perspective advances existing project risk management literature by empirically
demonstrating how hybrid governance and AI-enabled capabilities jointly influence outcomes.
From a theoretical standpoint, this study contributes to the growing body of hybrid project
management literature by validating a multidimensional model of risk governance that
incorporates AI analytics capability. It extends governance and dynamic capability theories
into the hybrid project domain, emphasizing the evolving role of AI in supporting
organizational control and adaptability. Practically, the findings suggest that organizations
managing hybrid projects should prioritize governance integration and process alignment
before investing heavily in visibility tools or escalation mechanisms. AI analytics initiatives
are most effective when embedded within coherent governance frameworks that support
informed and timely decision-making.
Overall, this discussion highlights that managing risk in hybrid project environments requires
more than adopting Agile practices or deploying advanced technologies in isolation. Instead,
organizations must focus on creating integrated governance structures, aligning risk processes,
and developing analytical capabilities that collectively support effective risk management in
complex and dynamic project settings.

20 CONCLUSION
This study set out to examine how governance mechanisms and artificial intelligence–driven
analytics influence risk management effectiveness within hybrid project environments. As
organizations increasingly adopt hybrid methodologies that combine Agile and traditional
approaches, managing project risk has become more complex due to fragmented governance
structures, divergent decision-making logics, and increasing environmental uncertainty. By
integrating governance consistency, risk process alignment, risk visibility, timely risk
escalation, and AI-driven risk analytics capability into a single structural model, this research
provides empirical insights into the factors that most strongly shape risk management outcomes
in hybrid projects.
Using a quantitative research design and structural equation modeling (SEM) based on data
collected from 384 project professionals, the study demonstrates that risk management
effectiveness in hybrid projects is primarily driven by structural coherence and organizational
capability rather than by information availability or procedural speed alone. Governance
consistency, risk process alignment, and AI-driven risk analytics capability emerged as
statistically significant predictors of effective risk management, while risk visibility and timely
risk escalation did not exhibit significant direct effects. These findings highlight the importance
of integrating governance structures and aligning risk processes across hybrid project settings,
while also recognizing the transformative role of AI analytics as an enabler of informed and
adaptive decision-making.
One of the most important contributions of this study lies in its empirical validation of
governance consistency as a foundational determinant of risk management effectiveness in
hybrid projects. Hybrid environments often operate under competing governance logics, where
Agile teams prioritize flexibility and decentralized authority, while traditional project
structures emphasize formal controls and hierarchical oversight. The findings indicate that
when governance mechanisms are applied consistently across these domains, organizations
experience improved accountability, clearer decision rights, and more coordinated risk
responses. This reinforces the view that hybrid project success does not arise from choosing
between Agile and traditional governance models, but from deliberately integrating them into
a coherent governance framework.

Risk process alignment also plays a critical role in enhancing risk management effectiveness.
The alignment of risk identification, assessment, response planning, and monitoring activities
across hybrid project components ensures that risk-related information flows consistently and
that responses are coordinated across teams and organizational levels. The findings suggest
that misaligned or fragmented risk processes undermine an organization’s ability to manage
uncertainty effectively, even when advanced tools or technologies are available. This
underscores the importance of viewing risk management as an end-to-end organizational
process rather than a collection of isolated practices.
A key insight from this research is the strategic significance of AI-driven risk analytics
capability. The results demonstrate that AI analytics is not merely a supportive technological
tool but functions as an organizational capability that enhances predictive foresight, decision
quality, and adaptive responsiveness. By continuously analyzing large volumes of project data,
AI systems enable early identification of emerging risks and support proactive intervention.
From a strategic perspective, AI-driven analytics can be understood as a capability that
complements and amplifies governance structures, reducing cognitive limitations and
enhancing situational awareness in complex project environments. This finding is particularly
relevant as organizations increasingly invest in digital transformation initiatives without fully
integrating them into governance and risk management frameworks.
Conversely, the non-significant findings related to risk visibility and timely risk escalation
challenge some conventional assumptions in project risk management literature. While
transparency and rapid escalation are often viewed as inherently beneficial, the results of this
study suggest that these factors do not directly translate into effective risk management
outcomes in hybrid environments. Risk visibility, while necessary, appears insufficient on its
own if organizations lack the governance clarity and interpretive capacity required to act on
available information. Similarly, timely risk escalation may be less critical in hybrid projects
where Agile teams are empowered to manage risks locally and where premature escalation can
disrupt workflows or undermine team autonomy. These findings suggest that risk management
effectiveness depends more on the quality of governance and decision-making than on the
speed or volume of information flows.
Taken together, the results of this study support an integrated view of risk management in
hybrid projects, where governance consistency, aligned processes, and analytical capabilities
jointly shape outcomes. Rather than treating risk management as a purely technical or

Effective risk management in hybrid environments requires deliberate governance design,
cross-method integration, and the development of capabilities that support adaptive decision-

From a theoretical perspective, this study contributes to the growing body of literature on
hybrid project management by empirically validating a multidimensional model of risk
governance that incorporates AI-driven analytics capability. While prior research has often
examined Agile and traditional risk management practices in isolation, this study advances
understanding by demonstrating how governance and technology interact within hybrid
contexts. The findings extend governance theory by highlighting the importance of consistency
across multiple project methodologies, and they contribute to dynamic capability theory by
positioning AI analytics as a capability that enhances organizational adaptability in uncertain
environments.
The study also contributes to the emerging literature on AI in project management by moving
beyond descriptive accounts of tool adoption and focusing on AI as a capability embedded
within governance and process structures. By empirically demonstrating the impact of AI
analytics capability on risk management effectiveness, this research provides a foundation for
future studies exploring the strategic role of AI in complex organizational systems.
Importantly, the findings caution against viewing AI as a standalone solution, emphasizing
instead the need for integration with governance frameworks and aligned processes.
From a practical standpoint, the findings offer several important implications for project
managers, organizations, and policymakers. Organizations managing hybrid projects should
prioritize the development of consistent governance frameworks that bridge Agile and
traditional approaches. Rather than allowing governance practices to evolve independently
within different project components, organizations should establish clear principles that define
decision rights, accountability, and escalation pathways across hybrid structures. This requires
not only formal policies but also cultural alignment and leadership commitment.
In addition, organizations should invest in aligning risk management processes across hybrid
project environments. This includes harmonizing risk identification methods, assessment
criteria, and monitoring mechanisms to ensure that risk information is comparable and

The findings also suggest that investments in AI-driven risk analytics should be accompanied
by parallel investments in governance and capability development. Simply deploying AI tools
without integrating them into decision-making processes and governance structures is unlikely
to yield meaningful improvements in risk management effectiveness. Organizations should
focus on developing the skills, routines, and organizational structures necessary to interpret
and act on AI-generated insights. This includes training project managers and decision-makers
to understand predictive analytics and to integrate AI outputs into strategic and operational
decisions.
Despite its contributions, this study is subject to several limitations that should be
acknowledged. First, the research adopts a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to
draw causal inferences or examine how risk management practices evolve over time.
Longitudinal studies could provide deeper insights into how governance mechanisms and AI
capabilities interact across different project phases. Second, the study focuses on a sample
drawn from India, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other geographic or
cultural contexts. While India provides a relevant context due to its widespread adoption of
hybrid project methodologies, future research could replicate the model in different regions to
assess its broader applicability.
Third, the study relies on self-reported perceptions of project professionals, which may be
subject to response bias. Although the sample size is adequate and the measurement model
demonstrates acceptable reliability and validity, future studies could complement survey data
with objective performance metrics or qualitative case studies. Additionally, while this study
focuses on direct relationships between constructs, future research could explore mediating or
moderating effects, such as organizational culture, project complexity, or regulatory
environments.
Future research could also extend this work by examining how AI-driven risk analytics
interacts with specific Agile practices, such as sprint planning or retrospectives, and with
traditional governance mechanisms, such as stage-gate reviews. Exploring the ethical and
organizational implications of AI adoption in project risk management, including issues related
to transparency, trust, and accountability, would further enrich understanding in this area.
