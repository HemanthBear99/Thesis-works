GENERAL INTRODUCTION
Project management has evolved considerably in recent years as organizations operate in increasingly complex, dynamic, and uncertain environments. Beyond the traditional focus on planning, scheduling, and technical execution, contemporary project management places greater emphasis on human-centered factors, including leadership practices, communication processes, and team dynamics. These factors play a critical role in shaping collaboration, engagement, and performance within project teams.
The widespread adoption of hybrid work arrangements has further transformed the way project teams’ function. Project team members often collaborate across physical and virtual spaces, relying heavily on digital communication tools and asynchronous interactions. While hybrid work models offer flexibility and access to diverse talent, they also introduce new managerial challenges related to coordination, trust-building, shared understanding, and conflict management. In such environments, the effectiveness of leadership and communication becomes central to ensuring that project teams remain aligned, motivated, and collaborative.
Despite the availability of advanced project management tools and standardized methodologies, many projects continue to face difficulties, including miscommunication, reduced team cohesion, unclear roles, and breakdowns in collaboration. These challenges are rarely caused by technical limitations alone but are often linked to how project managers lead teams, communicate with stakeholders, and manage interpersonal dynamics within hybrid settings. As a result, project success increasingly depends on project managers' ability to adopt human-centered approaches that address the social and relational dimensions of teamwork.
Academic literature highlights the growing importance of soft skills, emotional intelligence, inclusive leadership, and effective communication in project management. Prior studies suggest that human-centred leadership styles can positively influence team motivation, trust, and performance, particularly in diverse and distributed teams. However, while these themes are widely discussed, there remains a gap in empirical research examining how leadership and communication practices concretely shape team dynamics and collaboration in hybrid project teams operating within today’s evolving work environments.
This gap presents a significant managerial challenge. Project managers are expected to lead hybrid teams effectively, foster collaboration, and manage conflict. However, they often lack clear, evidence-based guidance on which leadership behaviours and communication strategies are most effective in practice. Without a deeper understanding of how human-centred leadership and communication influence team dynamics, organizations risk reduced project performance, lower team engagement, and weakened collaboration.
In response to this challenge, this thesis adopts an exploratory approach to examine human- centred leadership and communication in hybrid project teams. By focusing on team dynamics and collaboration, the study aims to provide insights into how project managers can better navigate the human complexities of hybrid work environments. Through qualitative investigation, this research seeks to contribute to both academic understanding and managerial practice by identifying patterns, challenges, and effective practices that support collaboration and team effectiveness in contemporary project management contexts.
Research Problem
How do human-centred leadership and communication practices influence team dynamics and thereby enhance collaboration in hybrid project teams?
Research Questions
To address the research problem, this study is guided by the following research questions:
RQ1. How do project managers describe and apply human-centered leadership practices within hybrid project teams?
RQ2. How are communication processes structured and experienced in hybrid project teams, and what challenges do they present for effective collaboration?
RQ3. How do leadership and communication practices influence team dynamics, including trust, motivation, and conflict management, in hybrid project environments?
RQ4. What practices are perceived by project managers as most effective in enhancing collaboration and cohesion within hybrid project teams?

CHAPTER 1
REVIEW OF SCIENTIFIC LITERATURE
Human-Centered Leadership in Project Management
Evolution of Leadership in Project Management
Leadership has long been recognized as a critical factor in project success. Early project management research primarily viewed leadership through a task-oriented and control-based lens, emphasizing planning accuracy, monitoring, and adherence to predefined schedules and budgets (Kerzner, 2025). In this traditional perspective, project managers were expected to act as coordinators and controllers, ensuring that tasks were executed efficiently and deviations were corrected promptly.
However, as projects have become increasingly complex, uncertain, and knowledge-intensive, scholars have questioned the adequacy of purely task-focused leadership approaches. Contemporary projects often involve diverse stakeholders, cross-functional teams, and high levels of interdependence, making rigid control mechanisms insufficient (Turner, 2014). As a result, leadership research in project management has progressively shifted toward more relational and people-centred perspectives.
Recent studies emphasize that effective project leadership extends beyond technical competence and procedural expertise to include interpersonal skills, emotional awareness, and the ability to motivate and engage team members (Huemann & Turner, 2024). In dynamic project environments, leaders are required to facilitate collaboration, manage conflicts, and create conditions that enable team members to contribute effectively. This shift reflects a broader transformation in management theory, which increasingly recognizes organizations as social systems rather than purely technical ones.
Furthermore, the rise of temporary and project-based organizations has reinforced the importance of leadership as a social influence process. Unlike traditional hierarchical structures, project teams often lack stable authority relationships, requiring leaders to rely more on influence, trust, and communication than on formal power (Aaltonen & Kujala, 2016). Consequently, leadership in project management has evolved toward a more human-centered orientation, focusing on relationships, shared goals, and team well-being.
Human-Centred and Inclusive Leadership Approaches
Human-centred leadership refers to leadership approaches that prioritize the needs, experiences, and development of individuals within teams. Rather than viewing team members solely as resources for task execution, human-centred leaders recognize them as active contributors whose motivation, emotions, and well-being significantly affect performance (Van Knippenberg & Sitkin, 2013). In project management contexts, this perspective is particularly relevant given the temporary nature of teams and the intensity of required collaboration. Several leadership theories align with human-centred principles. Transformational leadership, for instance, emphasizes inspiring and motivating followers by articulating a shared vision, providing individualized consideration, and fostering intellectual stimulation (Bass & Riggio, 2006). Empirical studies have shown that transformational leadership is positively associated with team motivation, trust, and performance in project environments (Akhavan Tabassi et al., 2025).
Servant leadership represents another influential human-centred approach. Servant leaders focus on supporting and empowering team members, removing obstacles, and facilitating personal growth (Bradley, 1999). In project teams, servant leadership has been linked to higher levels of trust, psychological safety, and collaboration, as leaders prioritize team needs over hierarchical control (Eva et al., 2019)
Ethical and inclusive leadership approaches further contribute to the human-centred paradigm. Ethical leadership emphasizes fairness, transparency, and integrity, which are critical for building trust in project teams operating under time pressure and uncertainty (Brown et al., 2005). Inclusive. Leadership, meanwhile, focuses on valuing diversity, encouraging participation, and ensuring that team members feel respected and heard (Randel et al., 2018). Such practices are particularly important in hybrid and diverse project teams, where differences in background, location, and communication styles can create barriers to collaboration.
Emotional intelligence is a central component across these leadership approaches. Leaders with high emotional intelligence are better equipped to recognize and manage their own emotions as well as those of others, enabling more effective communication and conflict management (Goleman et al., 2013). Research indicates that emotionally intelligent project leaders are more successful in fostering motivation, engagement, and psychological safety within their teams (Müller & Turner, 2010).
Overall, human-centred and inclusive leadership approaches shift the focus of project leadership from task control to relationship management, emphasizing the leader’s role in creating a supportive environment that enables collaboration and sustained performance.
Leadership Challenges in Hybrid Project Teams
While human-centred leadership offers significant benefits, its implementation becomes more challenging in hybrid project teams. Hybrid teams, characterized by a combination of remote and co-located work, alter traditional patterns of interaction and reduce opportunities for informal communication (Waizenegger et al., 2020). As a result, leaders must adapt their practices to maintain visibility, trust, and engagement across physical and virtual spaces.
One major challenge concerns trust-building. Trust in project teams is often developed through repeated interactions, informal exchanges, and shared experiences. In hybrid environments, reduced face-to-face contact can slow the development of trust and increase the risk of misunderstandings (Carter et al., 2020). Leaders must therefore adopt deliberate strategies, such as regular check-ins and transparent communication, to compensate for the lack of physical proximity.
Another challenge relates to empowerment and autonomy. Hybrid work arrangements often grant team members greater flexibility and independence, which can enhance motivation but also create ambiguity regarding roles and expectations. Human-centred leaders must balance autonomy with clarity by setting clear goals while allowing team members’ discretion in how tasks are performed (Contreras et al., 2020).
Additionally, leaders in hybrid project teams face difficulties in monitoring team dynamics and addressing conflicts. Conflicts may remain hidden longer in virtual settings, and non-verbal cues that signal disengagement or tension are harder to detect. Consequently, project leaders must rely more heavily on communication skills and emotional awareness to identify and address emerging issues before they escalate (AM, 2025)
These challenges highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of how human-centred leadership can be effectively enacted in hybrid project contexts. While existing literature acknowledges the importance of leadership in such environments, empirical insights into how project managers adapt their leadership practices to hybrid settings remain limited. Addressing this gap is essential for understanding how leadership can support team dynamics and collaboration in contemporary project management.
Communication in Hybrid Project Teams
Role of Communication in Project Success
Communication has long been recognized as a central determinant of project success. In project environments characterized by task interdependence, time pressure, and multiple stakeholders, communication serves as a key mechanism for coordination and alignment, enabling information sharing, decision-making, and collective action (Institute, 2000). Effective communication enables project teams to establish a shared understanding of objectives, roles, timelines, and expectations, thereby reducing ambiguity and the risk of errors.
In traditional co-located project teams, communication often occurs through a combination of formal mechanisms such as meetings, reports, and documentation, as well as informal interactions, including spontaneous conversations and social exchanges. These informal interactions play an important role in reinforcing relationships, building trust, and enabling rapid problem-solving (Methot et al., 2026). Research suggests that such informal communication channels contribute significantly to coordination efficiency and team cohesion in project settings. From a project management perspective, communication is also a critical leadership tool. Project managers rely on communication to articulate vision, align stakeholders, manage expectations, and motivate team members (Huemann & Turner, 2024). Leadership communication influences not only the transmission of information but also how team members interpret and act on messages. Clear, consistent, and transparent communication has been associated with higher levels of trust, commitment, and engagement within project teams (Men & Yue, 2019).
Stakeholder communication represents another essential dimension of project communication. Projects typically involve internal and external stakeholders with diverse interests, levels of power, and expectations. Effective communication helps project managers balance these interests, manage conflicts, and maintain stakeholder support throughout the project lifecycle (Aaltonen & Kujala, 2016). Inadequate communication, by contrast, has been identified as a leading cause of project failure, often resulting in misunderstandings, rework, and delays (Institute, 2000).
Overall, communication serves both instrumental and relational functions in project teams. Instrumentally, it enables task coordination and information exchange. Relationally, it supports trust-building, social integration, and collaboration. These dual functions become particularly salient in hybrid project teams, where digital technologies increasingly mediate communication.
Communication Challenges in Hybrid and Remote Teams
Hybrid project teams fundamentally alter communication patterns by reducing physical co- presence and increasing reliance on digital communication tools. While technologies such as email, instant messaging platforms, and video conferencing facilitate connectivity across distances, they also introduce new challenges that can undermine the effectiveness of communication (Waizenegger et al., 2020).
One prominent challenge in hybrid teams is digital communication overload. Team members may receive large volumes of messages across multiple platforms, leading to information fragmentation, reduced attention, and delayed responses (Mazmanian et al., 2013). In project environments where timely information exchange is critical, such overload can impair decision- making and coordination. Moreover, the absence of clear communication norms may exacerbate confusion regarding which channels to use for specific types of information. 
Misinterpretation and information loss represent additional challenges. Digital communication often lacks non-verbal cues such as tone, facial expressions, and body language, which play an important role in conveying meaning and emotional intent (Daft & Lengel, 1986). As a result, messages may be interpreted differently from their intended meaning, increasing the likelihood of misunderstandings and conflict. This risk is heightened in hybrid teams composed of members from diverse cultural and professional backgrounds.
Time zone differences and asynchronous work further complicate communication in hybrid project teams. When team members work in different locations and time zones, opportunities for real-time interaction are limited. Although asynchronous communication offers flexibility, it can slow feedback cycles and hinder rapid problem resolution (Dannals et al., 2021). Delays in communication may accumulate over time, negatively affecting project schedules and team morale.
Additionally, hybrid teams often experience reduced informal communication. The absence of spontaneous interactions, such as hallway conversations or informal check-ins, can weaken social bonds and make it more difficult for team members to raise concerns or share tacit knowledge (Carter et al., 2020). This reduction in informal exchanges may contribute to feelings of isolation and disengagement, particularly among remote team members.
These challenges suggest that communication in hybrid project teams requires deliberate management. Simply transferring traditional communication practices to digital platforms is often insufficient. Instead, project managers must adopt communication strategies that address the structural and social complexities introduced by hybrid work arrangements.
Human-Centred Communication Practices
In response to the challenges associated with hybrid work, recent literature emphasizes the importance of human-centred communication practices. Human-centred communication focuses not only on the efficient transmission of information but also on the relational and emotional aspects of interaction, recognizing communication as a social process that shapes trust, inclusion, and collaboration (Men & Yue, 2019).
Active listening and feedback are central elements of human-centred communication. Active Listening involves attentively engaging with others’ perspectives, asking clarifying questions, and     demonstrating empathy. In project teams, leaders who practice active listening are better positioned to understand team members’ concerns, identify emerging issues, and foster a sense of psychological safety (A. C. Edmondson & Lei, 2014). Constructive feedback, when delivered respectfully and transparently, further supports learning and continuous improvement.
Transparency and shared understanding are also critical in hybrid project teams. Transparent communication involves openly sharing information about project goals, decisions, and changes, thereby reducing uncertainty and speculation. Research indicates that transparency enhances trust and commitment, particularly in environments where team members have limited visibility into others’ work activities (Carter et al., 2020). Shared understanding, often referred to as “shared mental models,” enables team members to coordinate effectively by aligning their interpretations of tasks and roles (Mathieu et al., 2000).
Inclusive communication practices play a particularly important role in hybrid contexts. Inclusive communication ensures that all team members, regardless of location or status, have opportunities to contribute and be heard. This may involve deliberately inviting input from remote participants during meetings, rotating facilitation roles, or using asynchronous tools to gather perspectives (Randel et al., 2018). Inclusive communication practices help mitigate power imbalances and reduce the marginalization of remote team members.
Finally, leadership communication is central to sustaining collaboration in hybrid project teams. Leaders set the tone for communication by modelling openness, respect, and empathy. Studies suggest that leaders who communicate consistently, acknowledge uncertainty, and demonstrate concern for team well-being are more effective in maintaining engagement and cohesion in hybrid teams (Contreras et al., 2020).
Hybrid work does not affect all organizational units equally. Its impact is particularly pronounced in project-based structures, where collaboration depends on rapid trust formation, temporary authority arrangements, and intensive cross-functional coordination. Project teams therefore represent a critical setting in which to examine how leadership and communication practices shape team dynamics. In hybrid project environments, relational mechanisms are not supplementary they are foundational to effective coordination and performance.
Understanding Team Dynamics in Project Environments
Team dynamics refers to the patterns of interaction, relationships, and behaviours that emerge among team members as they work together toward shared objectives. In project environments, team dynamics are particularly important given the temporary nature of teams, high task interdependence, and the pressure associated with deadlines and performance expectations (Mathieu et al., 2017). Unlike permanent organizational teams, project teams are often formed for a limited duration and bring together individuals with diverse skills, backgrounds, and professional identities.
Classical theories of group development provide a useful foundation for understanding team dynamics. Tuckman’s model of group development, which outlines the stages of forming, storming, norming, performing, and adjourning, has been widely applied in project management research (Tuckman & Jensen, 1977). Although the linear progression suggested by this model has been critiqued, it remains valuable in illustrating how teams evolve and how conflict, cohesion, and performance are interrelated.
In project teams, roles, norms, and interdependencies strongly influence team dynamics. Clear role definition helps team members understand their responsibilities and how their tasks contribute to overall project goals, thereby reducing ambiguity and coordination breakdowns (Belbin, 2010). Norms related to communication, decision-making, and conflict resolution further shape how team members interact and collaborate. When roles and norms are poorly defined, project teams are more likely to experience tension, duplication of effort, and reduced efficiency.
Interdependence is another defining feature of project teams. Tasks are often sequential or reciprocal, meaning that the output of one team member becomes the input for another. High interdependence increases the need for coordination and mutual adjustment, making effective team dynamics essential for project success (Thompson, 1967). In hybrid project teams, where digital tools mediate interactions, managing these interdependencies becomes even more complex, highlighting the importance of leadership and communication practices that support collaboration. Trust, Motivation, and Psychological Safety
Trust is widely regarded as a foundational element of effective team dynamics and collaboration. In project teams, trust enables members to rely on one another’s competence and intentions, facilitating information sharing and cooperative behaviour (Costa et al., 2018). Trust is particularly critical in hybrid teams, where reduced visibility and limited face-to-face interaction can make it harder to assess others’ actions and commitment.
Research distinguishes between cognitive trust, based on perceptions of competence and reliability, and affective trust, grounded in interpersonal relationships and emotional bonds (McAllister, 1995). Both forms of trust contribute to collaboration in project teams. Cognitive trust supports task coordination, while affective trust enhances willingness to share concerns, admit mistakes, and provide mutual support. Studies indicate that leadership behaviours and communication practices play a central role in fostering both types of trust, especially in virtual and hybrid settings (Carter et al., 2020).
Motivation is another key component of team dynamics in project environments. Project-based work often involves high workloads, uncertainty, and tight deadlines, which can strain individual motivation over time. Motivation in project teams is influenced not only by extrinsic factors such as rewards and recognition but also by intrinsic factors, including autonomy, meaningful work, and a sense of belonging (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Human-centred leadership and communication practices can enhance intrinsic motivation by acknowledging individual contributions and supporting autonomy.
Psychological safety has emerged as a critical concept linking trust, motivation, and learning in teams. Psychological safety refers to a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, such as speaking up, asking questions, or challenging existing practices (A. Edmondson, 1999). In project teams, psychological safety enables members to raise concerns early, share innovative ideas, and address problems before they escalate. Empirical research demonstrates that psychological safety is positively associated with team learning, adaptability, and performance, particularly in complex and uncertain environments (A. C. Edmondson & Lei, 2014).
In hybrid project teams, maintaining psychological safety can be challenging due to reduced informal interaction and reliance on mediated communication. Leaders and project managers play a crucial role in signalling openness and respect, encouraging participation, and responding. constructively to input from team members. Without psychological safety, hybrid teams may experience silence, disengagement, and unresolved conflicts, undermining collaboration and project outcomes.
Conflict and Collaboration in Hybrid Teams
Conflict is an inevitable aspect of teamwork, particularly in project environments characterized by diversity, interdependence, and time pressure. Rather than viewing conflict solely as a negative phenomenon, contemporary research distinguishes between different types of conflict and their varying effects on team performance (De Dreu & Weingart, 2003).
Task conflict arises from differences in viewpoints, ideas, or opinions related to the content of the work. Moderate levels of task conflict can be beneficial, as they encourage critical thinking and improve decision quality. Process conflict, by contrast, involves disagreements about how work should be organized, including roles, responsibilities, and resource allocation. Relational conflict refers to interpersonal tensions and emotional disagreements, which are generally detrimental to collaboration and team well-being.
In hybrid project teams, conflict dynamics are shaped by communication constraints and reduced social cues. Misunderstandings may persist longer due to asynchronous communication, and relational conflicts may be harder to detect and resolve without face-to-face interaction (Dannals et al., 2021). Cultural diversity and differing communication styles can further intensify conflict if not managed effectively.
Conflict management approaches play a critical role in determining whether conflict undermines or enhances collaboration. Constructive conflict management involves open communication, mutual respect, and a focus on shared goals rather than personal interests (DeChurch et al., 2013). Leaders who encourage dialogue and facilitate problem-solving can help teams leverage task conflict while minimizing relational tension.
Collaboration in project teams emerges from the effective management of team dynamics, including trust, motivation, and conflict. Collaboration involves coordinated action, shared responsibility, and collective problem-solving, enabling teams to integrate diverse expertise and respond to challenges effectively (Kozlowski & Bell, 2013). In hybrid environments, collaboration depends heavily on leadership and communication practices that support alignment, inclusion, and continuous interaction.
Overall, team dynamics and collaboration are deeply intertwined in project teams. Understanding how trust, motivation, psychological safety, and conflict interact provides a foundation for examining how human-centred leadership and communication practices influence collaboration in hybrid project settings. This perspective reinforces the need for empirical investigation into the lived experiences of project managers and team members navigating these complex dynamics.
Hybrid Project Teams as a Contemporary Work Context
Managerial Implications of Hybrid Project Environments
The rise of hybrid project teams has significant managerial implications, particularly for project managers responsible for coordination, control, and team performance. One of the most prominent challenges concerns coordination and control. In hybrid environments, project managers have less direct visibility into daily work activities, making it more difficult to monitor progress and detect issues early (Wang et al., 2021). This shift requires a move away from presence-based control toward trust-based and outcome-oriented management.
Leadership adaptation is therefore essential in hybrid project settings. Project managers must develop new capabilities in virtual leadership, including building trust at a distance, communicating effectively through digital channels, and maintaining team engagement without relying on physical proximity (Contreras et al., 2020). Research suggests that leaders who fail to adapt their style may experience reduced team commitment and increased disengagement among remote team members.
Communication structures also require careful redesign in hybrid project teams. Informal communication, which traditionally played a key role in coordination and relationship-building, is less likely to occur spontaneously in hybrid settings. Project managers must therefore create intentional communication routines, such as regular check-ins, structured meetings, and clear documentation practices, to ensure information flows effectively (Waizenegger et al., 2020). Without such structures, hybrid teams risk fragmentation and misalignment.
Another important implication relates to equity and inclusion. Hybrid work arrangements can unintentionally create disparities between team members who are physically present more often and those who work remotely. Research indicates that remote team members may feel less visible and less involved in decision-making processes, which can undermine motivation and collaboration (Raghuram et al., 2019). Inclusive leadership and communication practices are therefore critical to ensuring that all team members have equal opportunities to contribute and be recognized.
Finally, hybrid project environments place greater emphasis on human-centred management practices. As discussed in previous sections, trust, psychological safety, and motivation are central to effective collaboration. In hybrid contexts, these elements cannot be taken for granted and must be actively cultivated through leadership behaviour and communication. Project managers who adopt human-centred approaches are better positioned to address the social and relational challenges of hybrid work, thereby enhancing team dynamics and collaboration.
In summary, hybrid project teams constitute a contemporary work context that reshapes traditional assumptions in project management. While hybrid work offers flexibility and efficiency, it also introduces new challenges related to coordination, communication, and leadership. Understanding these implications provides essential context for examining how human-centred leadership and communication practices can support collaboration in hybrid project teams, reinforcing the relevance of the empirical study that follows.
Tensions and Debates in Human-Centred Leadership and Trust
While the literature increasingly emphasizes the positive impact of human-centred leadership and inclusive communication practices on trust, motivation, and collaboration, scholarly debates highlight that these relationships are neither linear nor universally beneficial. Rather, leadership and team dynamics in complex project environments are shaped by inherent tensions and paradoxes that require careful navigation.
One prominent debate concerns the trust–control paradox. Traditional project management approaches rely on monitoring, structured governance, and performance controls to ensure adherence to timelines and budgets. In contrast, human-centred leadership emphasizes empowerment, autonomy, and relational trust. However, excessive reliance on trust without appropriate coordination mechanisms may create ambiguity and reduce accountability, particularly in technologically complex and high-risk projects. Scholars argue that effective leadership in such contexts involves balancing relational trust with structured oversight rather than replacing one with the other (Costa et al., 2018). This tension is particularly salient in hybrid environments, where reduced physical visibility can amplify concerns regarding performance monitoring and clarity of responsibility.
A related debate concerns psychological safety versus performance pressure. Psychological safety has been widely associated with learning, innovation, and knowledge sharing (Edmondson, 1999). However, some researchers caution that high levels of interpersonal comfort may unintentionally reduce performance intensity if not coupled with clear expectations and accountability structures. In project settings characterized by strict deadlines and technical interdependence, leaders must balance openness with performance discipline. Psychological safety, therefore, does not eliminate performance pressure but must coexist with it in a productive equilibrium.
The tension between inclusion and decision speed is another critical topic of debate in the literature. Inclusive leadership encourages broad participation and shared voice, particularly in cross-functional and diverse teams (Randel et al., 2018). However, extensive consultation may slow decision-making in time-sensitive project phases. In complex automotive automation projects, where integration milestones are tightly scheduled, leaders may face trade-offs between ensuring inclusive dialogue and maintaining rapid execution. The challenge lies in designing communication processes that are participative yet efficient.
Similarly, empowerment introduces the risk of accountability diffusion. When cross-functional ownership is emphasized and decision authority is distributed, responsibility boundaries can become blurred. While shared ownership strengthens collaboration and reduces silos, unclear accountability may result in duplicated effort or unresolved decisions. Research on team interdependence suggests that empowerment is most effective when accompanied by explicit role clarity and well-defined coordination mechanisms (Mathieu et al., 2017).
Finally, scholars increasingly recognize the dual role of conflict in team dynamics. While excessive relational conflict can undermine cohesion and trust (De Dreu & Weingart, 2003), moderate levels of task-related disagreement may enhance decision quality and innovation. The debate surrounding productive dissent versus excessive harmony challenges overly consensual portrayals of collaboration. In hybrid teams, where communication is mediated and informal interactions are reduced, dissent may either be suppressed or misinterpreted. Leaders must therefore cultivate environments in which constructive disagreement is encouraged, without allowing conflict to escalate into relational tension.
These tensions underscore that human-centred leadership and communication practices are not inherently unproblematic solutions. Rather, their effectiveness depends on how leaders navigate competing demands between autonomy and control, inclusion and efficiency, safety and accountability. Recognizing these debates provides a more nuanced understanding of collaboration in hybrid project teams. It reinforces the need for empirical investigation into how such tensions are experienced and managed in practice. Although existing research highlights the positive influence of human-centred leadership and communication on collaboration, the literature also reveals inherent tensions and trade-offs that remain insufficiently explored in hybrid project contexts.
Emergence of Hybrid Work in Project Management
Hybrid work has emerged as a dominant work arrangement in many organizations, particularly in project-based environments. Advances in digital technologies, globalization, and the growing emphasis on flexibility and work–life balance had already begun to reshape work practices prior to 2020. However, the COVID-19 pandemic significantly accelerated the adoption of hybrid and remote work models, transforming them from temporary solutions into long-term organizational strategies (Kniffin et al., 2021).
In project management contexts, hybrid work refers to arrangements in which project team members alternate between working remotely and on-site. This model enables organizations to access geographically dispersed talent, reduce operational costs, and offer greater flexibility to employees. At the same time, it alters traditional assumptions about coordination, supervision, and collaboration within project teams (Dannals et al., 2021).
Project-based work is particularly well-suited to hybrid arrangements due to its outcome-oriented nature. Projects are typically defined by clear objectives, timelines, and deliverables, which allows work to be structured around outputs rather than physical presence. As a result, many organizations have integrated hybrid work into project governance structures, relying on digital tools to manage communication, documentation, and performance (Institute, 2000).
Despite these advantages, the transition to hybrid work represents a profound shift in how project team’s function. Research indicates that hybrid teams experience both benefits and limitations. On the positive side, hybrid work can enhance autonomy, job satisfaction, and perceived work–life. Balance (Wang et al., 2021). On the negative side, it can weaken social cohesion, reduce informal learning, and increase the risk of communication breakdowns if not managed effectively.
These tensions highlight the need to reconsider traditional project management practices. While methodologies and tools remain important, the success of hybrid project teams increasingly depends on how leaders and team members navigate social interaction, communication norms, and shared responsibility across physical and virtual spaces.
Synthesis of the Literature and Identification of Research Gaps
The review of the scientific literature highlights a clear evolution in project management research toward a more human-centred perspective. Across the examined streams of literature, leadership, communication, and team dynamics consistently emerge as critical factors influencing collaboration and project success. Contemporary leadership research emphasizes the shift from task-oriented control toward relational, inclusive, and people-centred leadership approaches that prioritize trust, motivation, and psychological safety. Such approaches are increasingly recognized as essential in complex and dynamic project environments, particularly where formal authority is limited, and collaboration is critical.
Similarly, communication literature underscores its dual role in project teams: as a functional mechanism for coordination and information exchange, and as a relational process that shapes trust, engagement, and shared understanding. In hybrid project teams, communication challenges related to digital overload, misinterpretation, and reduced informal interaction are widely documented. Research suggests that human-centred communication practices, such as active listening, are beneficial. Listening, transparency, and inclusive participation can mitigate these challenges and support collaboration, yet their practical implementation remains underexplored.
The literature on team dynamics further reinforces the importance of trust, motivation, psychological safety, and constructive conflict management in enabling effective collaboration. While studies acknowledge that these elements are interdependent and influenced by leadership and communication, much of the existing research examines them in isolation or within traditional co-located teams. Hybrid project teams, by contrast, introduce additional complexity due to reduced physical proximity, reliance on digital tools, and altered social interaction patterns.
Despite the growing body of research on hybrid work, several gaps remain evident. First, there is limited empirical research that integrates human-centred leadership, communication practices, and team dynamics within a single analytical framework focused on hybrid project teams. Second, while theoretical models highlight the importance of trust, psychological safety, and collaboration, fewer studies examine how project managers enact these principles in practice within hybrid environments. Third, much of the existing literature remains conceptual or prescriptive, with insufficient qualitative insight into the lived experiences of project managers navigating hybrid team challenges.
These gaps point to the need for exploratory empirical research that captures how human-centred leadership and communication practices are experienced, interpreted, and applied in hybrid project contexts. Addressing these gaps can contribute to a more nuanced understanding of collaboration in contemporary project management and provide actionable insights for practitioners.

Definition and Operationalization of Key Concepts
Human-Centred Leadership
Human-centred leadership refers to leadership approaches that prioritize individuals’ needs, well-being, and development alongside task achievement. Recent leadership research emphasizes that effective leaders focus not only on performance outcomes but also on empathy, ethical behaviour,and the creation of supportive work environments (Bugarčić & Slavković, 2023). In project management contexts, where teams are temporary and interdependent, such leadership is particularly relevant.
The literature identifies several core dimensions of human-centred leadership. Empathy and emotional intelligence enable leaders to recognize and respond to team members’ emotional states, which is essential for maintaining engagement and psychological safety (Miao et al., 2017). Empowerment and autonomy involve granting team members discretion in how they perform tasks, thereby fostering intrinsic motivation and a sense of ownership (Breevaart & Zacher, 2019). Trust-building and ethical behaviour contribute to perceptions of fairness and reliability, which are crucial in project teams operating under uncertainty (Hoch et al., 2018).
In hybrid project teams, the role of project managers as human-centred leaders becomes even more critical. Reduced face-to-face interaction increases reliance on digitally mediated leadership behaviours, making empathy, clarity, and ethical conduct central to sustaining trust and collaboration (Contreras et al., 2020).
Communication in Hybrid Project Teams
Communication in project contexts refers to the processes through which information, meaning, and understanding are exchanged among team members and stakeholders. In hybrid project teams, communication is predominantly mediated by digital tools and occurs through both synchronous and asynchronous channels (Waizenegger et al., 2020).
The literature identifies several key dimensions of effective communication. Communication clarity ensures that messages are understood as intended, reducing ambiguity and rework (Institute, 2000). Frequency and feedback support alignment and enable timely correction of issues, particularly in fast-paced project environments (Dannals et al., 2021). Transparency and inclusiveness involve openly sharing information and ensuring that all team members, regardless of location, can participate in discussions and decision-making processes (Randel et al., 2018).
Human-centred communication practices emphasize not only efficiency but also relational quality. Active listening, respectful dialogue, and responsiveness contribute to trust and psychological safety, which are essential for collaboration in hybrid teams (Men & Yue, 2019). Without such practices, communication breakdowns may persist and undermine team dynamics.Team Dynamics in Hybrid Project Teams
Team dynamics refer to the patterns of interaction, relationships, and psychological states that develop within a team over time. In project environments, team dynamics are shaped by task interdependence, time pressure, and diversity of expertise (Mathieu et al., 2017).
Key elements of team dynamics include trust and psychological safety, motivation and engagement, and conflict and conflict management. Trust enables cooperation and knowledge sharing, while psychological safety encourages speaking up and learning behaviours (A. C. Edmondson & Lei, 2014). Motivation and engagement influence the level of effort and commitment team members bring to the project. Conflict, when managed constructively, can enhance decision quality, but unmanaged relational conflict can damage collaboration (DeChurch et al., 2013).
Hybrid work introduces specific challenges to team dynamics by reducing informal interaction and increasing dependence on mediated communication. As a result, team dynamics in hybrid project teams are particularly sensitive to leadership behaviour and communication quality (Carter et al., 2020).
Collaboration in Project Teams
Collaboration refers to the extent to which team members coordinate their efforts, share knowledge, and jointly solve problems to achieve shared goals. In project management, collaboration is a critical outcome that enables teams to integrate diverse expertise and respond effectively to uncertainty (Kozlowski & Bell, 2013).
Indicators of effective collaboration include coordination and alignment, shared understanding, and collective problem-solving. Collaborative teams demonstrate mutual accountability and adaptability, allowing them to manage interdependencies and deliver project outcomes efficiently (Guide, 2008). In this study, collaboration is conceptualized as an outcome influenced by leadership, communication, and team dynamics rather than as an isolated behaviour.

Relationships between Key Concepts
Influence of Human-Centred Leadership on Team Dynamics
Human-centred leadership has been shown to influence team dynamics significantly. Leadership behaviours that emphasize empathy, fairness, and support contribute to trust and psychological safety, enabling team members to engage openly and collaboratively. Empowering leadership practices enhance motivation by fostering autonomy and ownership, while ethical leadership reduces relational conflict and strengthens cohesion (Hoch et al., 2018).
In hybrid project teams, leadership influence is amplified due to reduced informal interaction. Project managers’ leadership behaviours serve as key signals that shape perceptions of trust, engagement, and conflict management (Contreras et al., 2020).
Role of Communication in Shaping Team Dynamics
Communication practices directly shape team dynamics by influencing shared understanding, trust, and engagement. Clear and transparent communication reduces ambiguity, while regular feedback supports alignment and learning (Dannals et al., 2021). In hybrid environments, communication also acts as a mediator of trust, compensating for the lack of physical proximity.
However, communication challenges such as digital overload and misinterpretation can undermine team dynamics if not addressed through human-centred practices (Waizenegger et al., 2020).
Joint Influence of Leadership and Communication on Collaboration
Leadership and communication interact synergistically to influence collaboration. Leadership intent is largely conveyed through communication, making alignment between leadership style and communication practices essential. Moreover, when human-centred leadership is supported by inclusive communication, team dynamics are strengthened, thereby increasing collaboration (Men & Yue, 2019).

Summary
Chapter 4 presents the qualitative findings derived from interview data exploring leadership, communication, coordination, conflict management, and managerial practices in cross-functional automotive automation projects operating under hybrid or distributed working arrangements. The results were structured into five major themes, each supported by direct participant quotations and summarized through frequency-based tables. Under Theme 1, findings revealed that leadership approaches were largely collaborative, inclusive, and adaptive, with respondents describing leaders as accessible and supportive while also maintaining structure and goal orientation. Leadership behaviors were consistently linked to trust-building, motivation, and improved collaboration, particularly when leaders enabled cross-functional participation, recognized contributions, and removed barriers rather than relying on micromanagement. However, the findings also highlighted that leadership effectiveness increased when technical understanding and decision clarity were present, especially in complex system integration contexts where ambiguity could otherwise create delays or reduce accountability.
Themes 2 and 3 highlighted that communication and coordination in hybrid environments rely heavily on formal mechanisms such as sprint meetings, stand-ups, milestone reviews, dashboards, and digital platforms like Teams and Jira. While these tools supported information flow and transparency, participants reported significant challenges including time zone delays, slower response times, reduced informal interactions, and weaker relationship-building. These barriers often contributed to misinterpretations, fragmented information, and difficulties in addressing urgent issues quickly. Theme 3 further emphasized that role alignment and responsibility clarity are critical in cross-functional projects, as misalignment frequently caused integration failures, duplicated work, rework, and delays in approvals. Theme 4 demonstrated that conflicts commonly emerge from task priorities, process differences, technical misunderstandings, decision authority ambiguity, and cultural communication differences. Teams typically resolved conflicts through weekly coordination meetings, joint problem-solving, agile retrospectives, and escalation only when necessary. Finally, Theme 5 showed that collaboration and adaptability were strengthened through cross-functional alignment meetings, clear ownership structures, shared dashboards, open feedback cultures, agile practices, and psychological safety. Respondents strongly recommended that project and product managers focus on transparency, trust-building, role clarity, equal inclusion of remote members, proactive conflict handling, and balancing performance demands with team well-being to sustain long-term hybrid team success.
CHAPTER-5
Discussion and Conclusion
Discussion
The findings of this study provide empirical support for the analytical framework developed in Chapter 2, demonstrating that human-centred leadership and communication practices significantly influence collaboration in hybrid automotive automation project teams through their impact on team dynamics. The results indicate that leadership and communication do not directly generate collaboration; rather, they shape the psychological and relational conditions such as trust, motivation, psychological safety, and conflict management through which collaboration becomes possible. In hybrid project environments characterized by technological complexity, cross-functional interdependence, and reduced face-to-face interaction, these human-centred managerial behaviours function as critical stabilizing mechanisms that compensate for structural fragmentation and communication constraints inherent in distributed work arrangements.
The analysis revealed that effective leadership in hybrid automotive automation projects extends beyond traditional task-oriented control and performance monitoring. Participants consistently emphasized accessibility, transparency, empathy, and supportive engagement as defining characteristics of impactful leadership. This aligns with contemporary leadership literature, which highlights a shift from directive and control-based project management toward relational and empowerment-oriented leadership approaches. However, the present findings add contextual depth by demonstrating that in hybrid settings, leadership behaviours carry amplified significance due to reduced informal interaction. In co-located teams, trust and cohesion often develop organically through daily social contact. In hybrid environments, such spontaneous relational development is limited, and leadership behaviours become primary signals shaping perceptions of fairness, reliability, and inclusion. Leaders who maintained regular engagement, clarified expectations, and encouraged open dialogue were more likely to foster psychological safety and motivation among cross-functional team members.
The findings further suggest that empowerment and autonomy are particularly important in hybrid automotive projects, where teams must adapt to rapidly evolving technological requirements and interdependent workflows. Leaders who provided strategic clarity while allowing discretion in execution enhanced intrinsic motivation and collective ownership. Conversely, overly directive leadership styles were associated with reduced initiative and diminished engagement. This supports existing theoretical perspectives on transformational and servant leadership while reinforcing the proposition that human-centred leadership positively influences team dynamics in hybrid project contexts.
Communication practices emerged as equally central to collaborative effectiveness. Participants described communication not only as a mechanism for transmitting information but also as a relational process shaping alignment and trust. Hybrid work arrangements increase reliance on digital platforms, yet the findings indicate that technology alone does not ensure coordination. Where communication lacked clarity, consistency, or transparency, teams experienced duplication of effort, misunderstandings, and delays in decision-making. Structured communication routines such as regular cross-functional alignment meetings and clearly defined information channels were perceived as essential in maintaining shared understanding. These findings reinforce established communication theories emphasizing the importance of feedback loops and clarity in reducing ambiguity, particularly in environments with limited non-verbal cues.
Importantly, inclusive communication practices played a critical role in hybrid team dynamics. Participants noted that remote members could feel less visible or less involved in decision-making processes if discussions were dominated by those physically present. Leaders who actively invited contributions from all participants and ensured equitable information access mitigated these risks. This finding extends existing hybrid work literature by demonstrating that inclusiveness is not only an ethical imperative but also a functional requirement for sustaining engagement and collaboration across distributed teams.
The mediating role of team dynamics was strongly supported by the empirical data. Trust emerged as a foundational element enabling effective cross-functional collaboration. Both cognitive trusts, grounded in perceptions of competence, and affective trust, based on interpersonal relationships, were identified as critical. In hybrid automotive projects, trust required deliberate reinforcement through consistent communication and ethical leadership behaviours. Psychological safety was similarly central, particularly in complex automation projects where rapid iteration and problem detection are common. Teams characterized by high psychological safety were more willing to raise technical concerns early, share uncertainties, and collaboratively solve problems. In contrast, environments lacking such safety experienced prolonged issue resolution and reduced adaptive capacity.
Conflict dynamics further illustrated the mediating influence of team processes. Task-related disagreements were often constructive when managed through open dialogue and shared problem-solving. However, relational tensions intensified when communication breakdowns persisted or when digital interactions were misinterpreted. Effective leaders reframed conflicts around shared project objectives, thereby preventing escalation and preserving cohesion. These observations confirm that leadership and communication practices shape the internal relational climate of teams, which in turn determines the quality of collaboration.
Collaboration itself emerged as an outcome characterized by coordinated action, shared ownership, adaptive decision-making, and collective problem-solving across engineering, quality, operations, and product management functions. Rather than being a static attribute, collaboration appeared as an emergent property resulting from aligned leadership, structured communication, and positive team dynamics. Projects where human-centred practices were consistently applied demonstrated greater resilience in responding to shifting requirements and technological uncertainty. This finding reinforces the proposition that positive team dynamics enhance collaboration and underscores the strategic importance of relational competencies in hybrid project environments.
From a theoretical perspective, this study contributes to project management research by empirically validating an integrated framework linking human-centred leadership, communication practices, team dynamics, and collaboration within hybrid automotive automation contexts. While prior literature has examined these constructs individually, the present findings demonstrate their interdependence and highlight the mediating role of team dynamics. Moreover, the study reframes leadership in hybrid environments as a relational stabilizer that compensates for reduced informal interaction and fragmented communication structures.
Managerially, the findings suggest that project managers in hybrid automotive automation environments should prioritize trust-building behaviours, establish clear and inclusive communication routines, balance autonomy with strategic alignment, and proactively address relational tensions. Technical competence and methodological rigor remain important, but they are insufficient without deliberate cultivation of the human dimension of project management.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. The qualitative design and purposive sampling strategy limit generalizability beyond similar automotive automation contexts. Additionally, the findings reflect participant perceptions and may be influenced by organizational culture or role-specific perspectives. Future research could extend this work through longitudinal studies examining trust development over time or through quantitative testing of the proposed framework across industries.
Overall, the discussion confirms that collaboration in hybrid automotive automation projects is fundamentally shaped by human-centred leadership and communication practices. In technologically intensive and distributed work environments, the social and relational dimensions of project management emerge as decisive factors in sustaining trust, adaptability, and performance. These findings reinforce the central argument of this thesis: that effective collaboration in contemporary hybrid project teams depends not only on technical systems and processes but, critically, on the quality of leadership and communication that shape team dynamics.
Conclusion
The present study set out to explore how human-centered leadership and communication practices influence team dynamics and collaboration within hybrid automotive automation project teams. Drawing on a qualitative research design grounded in semi-structured interviews with experienced professionals across product management, engineering, quality assurance, and operations, the research sought to integrate theoretical insights with lived managerial experiences. The findings provide a coherent and empirically supported response to the research problem and questions, while also contributing to contemporary debates on hybrid work, project leadership, and cross-functional collaboration.
At its core, this thesis demonstrates that collaboration in hybrid project environments does not emerge automatically from technical structures, digital tools, or formal governance mechanisms. Instead, collaboration is a relational outcome shaped by leadership behaviors and communication practices that influence internal team dynamics. The empirical results confirm the analytical framework developed in Part 1, particularly the mediating role of team dynamics between human-centered leadership and communication on one side, and collaborative effectiveness on the other. Leadership and communication practices were shown to affect trust, psychological safety, motivation, and conflict management processes, which in turn determined the extent to which cross-functional teams could coordinate effectively, adapt to uncertainty, and collectively solve problems.
One of the central conclusions of this study is that hybrid project environments amplify the importance of relational competence in leadership. Reduced face-to-face interaction limits informal trust-building and spontaneous alignment. As a result, leadership behaviors become more visible and more consequential. Participants consistently emphasized the value of leader accessibility, transparency, ethical conduct, and empowerment. These behaviors were associated with stronger trust and engagement, particularly in distributed teams where visibility of effort and intention may otherwise be limited. The findings therefore suggest that in hybrid automotive automation projects, leadership operates less as a supervisory mechanism and more as a relational stabilizer that compensates for structural fragmentation.
Similarly, communication practices were found to perform both instrumental and relational functions. While formal communication mechanisms ensured coordination of tasks and dependencies across engineering and operational functions, relational aspects of communication such as tone, inclusiveness, responsiveness, and clarity shaped psychological safety and mutual understanding. Hybrid settings, characterized by reliance on digital communication platforms, increased the risk of misinterpretation and information overload. The study demonstrates that deliberate structuring of communication routines, transparent information sharing, and inclusive dialogue are essential for sustaining alignment and minimizing conflict. In this sense, communication in hybrid project teams is not merely a channel for transmitting information but a mechanism for constructing shared meaning and reinforcing collective responsibility.
The research further confirms that team dynamics constitute the central mechanism through which leadership and communication translate into collaborative outcomes. Trust both cognitive and affective emerged as foundational to effective cross-functional interaction. Psychological safety was particularly critical in technology-intensive automotive automation projects, where rapid problem detection, iterative development, and innovation require team members to speak openly about uncertainties and risks. Where leaders modeled openness and encouraged dialogue, teams demonstrated greater adaptability and resilience. Conversely, where psychological safety was weak, conflicts persisted longer, and collaboration deteriorated.
Conflict dynamics provided additional insight into the complexity of hybrid teamwork. Task-related disagreements were often productive when managed constructively, contributing to improved decision-making and technical rigor. However, relational conflicts intensified in contexts of unclear communication or misaligned expectations. Effective conflict management required both structured dialogue and leader intervention focused on shared goals rather than individual positions. This underscores the interdependent nature of leadership, communication, and team processes in determining collaborative quality.
Beyond validating the proposed analytical framework, this study contributes to theory by offering an integrated perspective that connects human-centered leadership, communication practices, team dynamics, and collaboration within hybrid project contexts. While existing literature has examined these constructs independently, this research demonstrates their systemic interaction in technologically complex environments. The findings also extend hybrid work literature by highlighting the heightened strategic importance of relational leadership in distributed project settings, particularly in industries characterized by high task interdependence and rapid technological change.
From a managerial standpoint, the conclusions of this study emphasize that effective hybrid project management requires intentional cultivation of the human dimension of teamwork. Project managers must move beyond a narrow focus on technical coordination and adopt practices that actively build trust, clarify expectations, encourage inclusive participation, and address relational tensions early. Structured communication routines, clear role definitions, and empowerment balanced with accountability were identified as practical mechanisms for enhancing collaboration. Organizations operating in hybrid automotive automation environments should therefore invest in leadership development programs that strengthen emotional intelligence, inclusive communication skills, and conflict facilitation competencies.
It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this research. The qualitative design, while enabling rich contextual insight, limits statistical generalization. The purposive sampling strategy focused on experienced professionals within automotive automation contexts, and findings may differ in other industries or cultural environments. Additionally, the study captures perceptions at a particular point in time; longitudinal research could further illuminate how trust and collaboration evolve across project lifecycles. Nevertheless, the depth and consistency of the data provide a robust foundation for conceptual insights and practical implications.
Future research may build upon this work by quantitatively testing the proposed framework across diverse sectors, comparing hybrid and fully remote project teams, or examining the role of digital collaboration tools in shaping psychological safety. Comparative cross-cultural studies may also deepen understanding of how leadership and communication practices operate in different institutional settings. Such research would further refine the understanding of human-centered management in contemporary project environments.
In conclusion, this thesis affirms that the success of hybrid automotive automation projects depends not solely on technological infrastructure or methodological rigor, but fundamentally on the quality of human interaction shaped by leadership and communication practices. In environments marked by distributed work arrangements, complex interdependencies, and rapid technological evolution, collaboration emerges as a relational achievement rather than a procedural outcome. Human-centered leadership and communication are therefore not supplementary soft skills, but strategic capabilities essential to sustaining trust, adaptability, and performance in hybrid project teams. Through its integrated analytical framework and qualitative empirical insights, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of project management in the digital age and reinforces the enduring importance of the human dimension in organizational effectiveness.
